Amulet · bench rig · skin conductance channel

EDA Electrode Wiring

Skin conductance is the same divider as the force strip, with your skin as the variable resistor. Two of the ECG snap electrodes go on one hand, a fixed resistor from your drawer turns the skin's resistance into a voltage, and the XIAO reads it on D3. Nothing on the board needs to move.

Sensor 2 × ECG snap electrodes · Ag/AgCl, pre-gelled Reads on D3 / A3 Divider 100 kΩ to GND (220 kΩ for dry hands) Expect 2–20 µS · 150–700 counts

Three things that are different from the force strip

  • The resistor is much bigger. Skin between two fingers sits at 50 kΩ to 500 kΩ. The 10 kΩ on the FSR would squash the whole signal into the bottom 5 % of the ADC. Use 100 kΩ.
  • Both electrodes go on the same hand. The current path must be skin-to-skin through the palm, not across the body. Two adjacent sites, a few centimetres apart.
  • Current is microamps. With 100 kΩ in series the worst case is 33 µA, far below anything you can feel. Keep the laptop on battery while you test if you want to be tidy about it; it isn't required.

Read the bands with the gold or silver band on the right. The third band is the multiplier: orange is ×1 000, yellow is ×10 000, green is ×100 000. So "brown black yellow" is 10 × 10 000 = 100 kΩ. If the reading with 100 kΩ sits under 150 counts after two minutes on the skin, swap to 220 kΩ. If it sits above 800, go down to 47 kΩ.

The snap studs take a standard 3.9 mm ECG snap lead. If the Amazon pack didn't come with leads, a small alligator clip on the stud is fine for the bench, then AWG30 silicone wire to the breadboard. Keep both leads short and away from the amp's I²S wires.

Where to put the electrodes

Skin conductance comes from eccrine sweat glands, and those respond to arousal rather than heat. The palms and fingertips have by far the densest supply, roughly three to four times the forearm, so that is where the signal is. Anywhere else on the body is a compromise.

A · best Index and middle fingertip pads

The gel disc centred on the pad of the last segment of each finger, palm side. This is the densest sweat-gland site on the body and the standard research placement. Trim the foam backing with scissors so the disc fits the fingertip; the electrode still works with a smaller foam ring. Tape lightly if it lifts.

Why: largest responses, fastest rise, cleanest baseline. Reacts within 1–3 s to a deep breath or a sudden noise.

B · alternative Thenar and hypothenar mounds

One pad on the fleshy mound below the thumb, the other on the mound below the little finger, both on the palm. The foam pads fit here without trimming and stay put if the hand rests on a desk.

Why: nearly as responsive as fingertips, less prone to lifting when you type or hold something. Slightly smaller responses.

C · fallback Middle segments of the same fingers

One joint down from site A, on the palm side. Use it only if both fingertips are spoken for. Responses are about half the size of the fingertip.

Why: keeps the fingertips free for the pulse sensor when only one hand is available.

Skin prep: water only, then wait. Wash the hand with plain water and dry it. No alcohol wipes: alcohol strips the surface moisture and flattens the signal for 10–15 minutes. Press the pads on and give the gel two to three minutes to settle before reading anything. The reading will creep upward for the first quarter hour as the gel hydrates the skin; that is normal with ECG gel. Keep the hand still, warm, and resting on the desk.

Do it

  1. Power off. Unplug the USB-C from the XIAO. The rails stay as they are.
  2. Pick a free breadboard row on the left side for the junction. It needs four holes, so choose one clear of the FSR junction.
  3. Bend the 100 kΩ legs down. One leg into the junction row, the other into the − rail.
  4. Same for a 100 nF cap: one leg junction row, one leg − rail. It has no polarity.
  5. Teal jumper from the junction row to D3. D3 is the fourth pin down on the left edge; D2 above it is still free.
  6. Two leads to the electrodes. Snap or clip lead A, other end into the + rail. Snap or clip lead B, other end into the junction row.
  7. Wash and dry the left hand, press the electrodes on at site A, and start a two-minute timer.
  8. Plug the USB-C back in. The bridge reconnects on its own.

What you'll see

1
Electrodes off, dangling: 0–5 counts.
Open circuit. The pull-down holds D3 at ground. This is the "nothing connected" reading.
2
Snap studs touched together: 1000+.
A short across the divider. Proves both leads and the rail are good.
3
On skin, two minutes in: 150–700, drifting slowly.
That slow wander is the tonic level. It moves with mood and temperature over minutes.
4
Take a sudden deep breath: a bump of 20–60 counts.
Starts 1–3 s after the breath, peaks in about 2 s, fades over 5–10 s. That bump is the skin conductance response, the thing this channel exists to catch.
5
Stuck at 0 with electrodes on: a lead is open.
Reseat the snap, check the clip is on the metal stud and not the foam.
